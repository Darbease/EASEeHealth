PROOFPA — VIDEO DEMO SCRIPT
============================

Target length: 8–12 minutes
Format: Screen recording with voiceover, terminal + browser split

────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 1: INTRO (0:00 – 1:00)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[SCREEN: README hero section or architecture diagram]

VOICEOVER:

"ProofPA is a privacy-preserving prior authorization system built on
Chainlink CRE — the Compute Runtime Environment. It connects
healthcare providers, payers, and patients through a pipeline where
no protected health information ever touches the blockchain or
oracle network logs.

The system has three layers:
  - Six backend Express services that hold clinical data
  - Five CRE workflows that orchestrate decisions inside a
    decentralized oracle network
  - Five Solidity contracts on-chain that enforce state transitions
    and settle payouts in mock USDC

Every sensitive HTTP call is AES-GCM encrypted end-to-end through
the DON. The blockchain only sees hashes, state transitions, and
payout amounts — never a patient name, diagnosis, or MRN.

Let me show you how it works."

SECTION 2: ARCHITECTURE WALKTHROUGH (1:00 – 2:30)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[SCREEN: Scroll through the system wiremap in the README]

VOICEOVER:

"Here's the full system wiremap. On the left, six Express services
running on ports 3001 through 3006 — policy, credentials, proof
evaluation, consent management, the provider adapter API, and
decision callbacks.

In the center, the Chainlink CRE DON runs five workflows.
WF-001 is the main prior auth flow — it makes ten calls: one
regular HTTP with DON consensus, two confidential encrypted calls,
two on-chain reads, and five on-chain writes. That's policy lookup,
consent verification, proof evaluation, claim submission, proof
recording, payout scheduling, payout release, marking the claim
PAID, and provider notification — all in a single workflow execution.

WF-002 monitors consent revocations — and writes revocations
on-chain if the ConsentRegistry is out of sync. WF-003 handles
the full challenge lifecycle: challengeClaim, resolveChallenge,
and cancelPayout if denied.
WF-004 is pure on-chain — it reads claim and escrow state to detect
stuck claims and settlement mismatches, no HTTP at all. WF-005
showcases AES-GCM encryption with four encrypted calls in sequence.

At the bottom, five Solidity contracts on Base Sepolia — or local
Anvil for this demo. MockUSDC for the settlement token,
ConsentRegistry, PolicyRegistry, ClaimDecisionRegistry for the
state machine, and ClaimEscrow for payouts.

Let's start everything up."

SECTION 4: SCENARIO A — HAPPY PATH (3:30 – 5:30)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[SCREEN: Terminal — run demo or workflow simulation]

VOICEOVER:

"Scenario A is the happy path. A provider submits a prior auth
request for a knee MRI. The system needs to verify four things:
is the provider credentialed, is the patient's consent active,
does the policy cover this procedure, and is the amount within
the plan cap?

I'll run WF-001 — the prior auth decision workflow."

  $ make simulate-wf001

[SCREEN: Highlight each log line as it appears]

"Step 1: The workflow fetches the policy from the policy service.
It gets back the predicates — covered procedures, amount caps,
attestation window. Notice the log says 'predicates loaded' — it
doesn't echo what those predicates are. No clinical data in the
CRE logs.

Step 2: On-chain consent check. It calls ConsentRegistry dot
isConsentActive and gets back true — consent is active.

Step 3: On-chain policy check. PolicyRegistry confirms the policy
hash is active.

Step 4: This is the confidential call. The workflow sends the
claim details plus the policy predicates it got from step 1 to
the proof service — encrypted with AES-GCM. The proof service
evaluates six predicate checks: credential valid, procedure
covered, amount within cap, consent active, not a duplicate,
attestation is fresh. All six pass.

Step 5: Submit the claim on-chain. ClaimDecisionRegistry transitions
the state from NONE to SUBMITTED. This is a DON-signed write —
the report is signed by the oracle network and submitted as a
transaction.

Step 6: Record the proof result. State goes from SUBMITTED to
APPROVED, reason bitmap is zero — no denial flags.

Step 7: Schedule the payout. ClaimEscrow stages 85,000 units of
mock USDC to the treasury address. The payout status goes from
NONE to SCHEDULED.

Step 8: Release the payout. ClaimEscrow executes the actual
ERC-20 transfer from escrow to treasury. Status goes from
SCHEDULED to RELEASED.

Step 9: Mark the claim PAID — terminal state. No further
transitions allowed. The claim lifecycle is complete on-chain.

Step 10: Notify the provider via encrypted callback. Decision:
APPROVED.

The entire flow — policy lookup, two on-chain reads, encrypted
proof evaluation, five on-chain writes, and provider notification
— executed in a single CRE workflow run."

[OPTIONAL: Run broadcast mode to show on-chain writes]

"With the broadcast flag, those EVM writes actually land on Anvil.

"State is 6 — that's PAID. The claim went all the way through:
submitted, approved, payout scheduled, released, and marked paid.
The full lifecycle completed on-chain."

SECTION 5: SCENARIO B — REVOKED CONSENT (5:30 – 7:00)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[SCREEN: Terminal]

VOICEOVER:

"Scenario B: what happens when a patient revokes consent? This
triggers two things — the proof service should deny the claim
with bit 3 set in the reason bitmap, and WF-002 should detect
the revocation and flag affected claims.

First, the demo runner revokes consent and re-evaluates."

  $ make demo
  (show Scenario B output)

"The proof service returns FAIL with bitmap 8 — that's bit 3,
consent invalid. Five of six checks passed, but consent is the
dealbreaker. The claim is DENIED.

Now let's run WF-002 — the consent revocation monitor."

  $ make simulate-wf002

"WF-002 polls the consent service for recent revocations, gets
back the data, then cross-checks on-chain. It calls
ConsentRegistry.isConsentActive — if the chain says ACTIVE but the
service says REVOKED, the workflow writes revokeConsent on-chain
to synchronize the state. If already revoked, it skips the write.

Then it notifies the callback service to flag pending claims.
Notice again — the logs show consent hash prefix and counts, not
patient names."

SECTION 6: SCENARIO C — CHALLENGE (7:00 – 8:30)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[SCREEN: Terminal]

VOICEOVER:

"Scenario C: an approved claim gets challenged. An ops reviewer
flags the claim — maybe the amount seems high for the procedure.
The payout is immediately blocked.

Let's run WF-003 to see the challenge resolution monitor."

  $ make simulate-wf003

"The workflow polls for pending challenges. It finds one —
one challenge for the claim, flagged by the clinical review team.
It reads the on-chain state from ClaimDecisionRegistry to verify
the claim is APPROVED.

Now the on-chain writes. Step 3: challengeClaim — the claim
transitions from APPROVED to CHALLENGED. The payout is blocked.

Step 4: resolveChallenge — the workflow resolves the challenge.
If approved, the claim goes back to APPROVED and the payout
unblocks. If denied, step 5 fires: cancelPayout on ClaimEscrow,
which returns the staged funds to the pool.

Finally it notifies the provider via encrypted callback.

This is the full state machine in action — three on-chain writes
in a single workflow: challenge, resolve, and conditionally cancel
the payout."

"WF-004 is the watchdog. It runs on a schedule — every 30 seconds

It checks two things: is any claim stuck in PROOF_PENDING beyond the

  $ make simulate-wf004

"It reads the claim state — APPROVED, last updated at this

is the expected value. No mismatches, no stuck claims. All clear.

If something were wrong — say a claim had been in PROOF_PENDING for
20 minutes — it would flag an SLO violation right here."

SECTION 8: WF-005 ENCRYPTION SHOWCASE (9:30 – 10:30)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[SCREEN: Terminal]

VOICEOVER:

"WF-005 is the encryption showcase. It makes four confidential HTTP
calls — every single one with encryptOutput true — chained with one
on-chain consent check. This is the full credential audit pipeline.

Step 1: verify provider credentials — encrypted.
Step 2: check consent on-chain — this is an EVM read, not HTTP.
Step 3: fetch the policy — encrypted. The predicates from this
response get piped directly into step 4.
Step 4: evaluate medical necessity — encrypted. Uses the predicates
from step 3, not hardcoded values.
Step 5: post audit results — encrypted.

Four out of four HTTP calls encrypted with AES-GCM."

  $ make simulate-wf005

"Audit result: PASS. All steps completed, all responses encrypted
end-to-end through the DON."

SECTION 9: PRIVACY & DESIGN (10:30 – 11:30)
────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

[SCREEN: Scroll through CRE logs from any workflow run]

VOICEOVER:

"Let me highlight something you may have noticed. Look at these CRE
logs. You see claim hashes, consent hashes, procedure codes like
PROC_KNEE_MRI, predicate pass-fail counts, on-chain state names.

What you don't see: no patient names, no MRNs, no diagnoses, no
dollar amounts, no clinical narrative. The CRE workflows parse
service responses for business logic — they use the policy
predicates to evaluate the proof, they check consent status — but
they never echo that data back to the DON logs.

The services hold the clinical data. The CRE DON orchestrates the
decisions. The blockchain enforces the state machine. Each layer
sees only what it needs.

This is the core privacy guarantee: the on-chain record is a
verifiable audit trail of decisions and settlements, without
revealing who the patient is or what their diagnosis was."

PRODUCTION NOTES
================

Terminal setup:
  - Font size 16+ for readability on recording
  - Dark terminal theme with high contrast
  - Split pane: left for commands, right for log output

Commands to run before recording:
  1. make anvil          (terminal 1, leave running)
  2. make deploy-local   (terminal 2)
  3. make services       (terminal 3, leave running)
  4. Clear terminal 2 for the demo commands

Key moments to emphasize:
  - Step 4 of WF-001: "predicates loaded" vs. never showing what they are
  - WF-001 steps 7-9: schedule → release → markPaid — full payout lifecycle
  - The bitmap: 0 = approved, 8 = consent denied (bit 3)
  - WF-002: revokeConsent on-chain write when state is out of sync
  - WF-003: three on-chain writes — challenge, resolve, cancel payout
  - WF-004 has zero HTTP calls — pure on-chain health check
  - WF-005 four-for-four encrypted calls
  - Every log line: hashes and codes, never names

If time is tight, cut sections 7–8 (WF-004/005) and go straight
from Scenario C to Privacy & Design. Core story is WF-001 + the
three scenarios.
